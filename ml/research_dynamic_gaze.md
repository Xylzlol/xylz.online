# Dynamic gaze research: full-field pours and in-letter fluid motion

## 1. Eyes during a large expanding front / full-field transition

**Smooth pursuit is not a candidate for a 1500-4000 px/s edge-type front.** Pursuit gain (eye velocity / target velocity) stays near 1.0 only up to roughly 30 deg/s for a small discrete target, degrading sharply above that, with an absolute ceiling near 70-100 deg/s only for brief, favorable conditions (Meyer, Lasker & Robinson 1985; summarized in Wikipedia "Smooth pursuit," and `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2928002`). A 1500-4000 px/s front, at typical screen viewing distances, is far above this — and more importantly, pursuit requires a trackable *point* or *edge feature*, not an expanding, textureless flood with no persistent single target. Pursuit latency is ~100-150 ms, open-loop (no feedback) for the first ~100 ms (Wikipedia "Smooth pursuit"; `eneuro.org/.../ENEURO.0343-16.2017`). Pursuit gain also depends on target size: gain increases with target size up to a point, but a large, edge-less object does not support pursuit the way a small dot does, and catch-up-saccade frequency rises when there is no small foveal feature to lock onto (Heinen et al. 2016, "A Foveal Target Increases Catch-Up Saccade Frequency During Smooth Pursuit," `ski.org`; Wright State repository). **Unverified**: exact gain figures for a full-field expanding color front specifically (no study tests this stimulus class directly).

**What actually happens: a fast, largely reflexive orienting saccade toward the point of onset, then fixation/ambient viewing of the spreading front, not sustained pursuit.** The relevant mechanisms:

- **Abrupt onset / new-object capture**: Yantis & Jonides (1984, *JEP:HPP*) and Jonides & Yantis (1988) showed abrupt onsets (new perceptual objects) capture attention and elicit reflexive saccades even when task-irrelevant; color/luminance singletons alone do not have the same automatic pull (ResearchGate PDF of Yantis 1984; `psych.unl.edu/.../Yantis.pdf`).
- **Motion-onset capture**: Abrams & Christ (2003), "Motion onset captures attention," *Psychological Science* 14(5) (PubMed 12930472; Abrams lab PDF at `openscholarship.wustl.edu/art_sci_etds/1174`), demonstrated that the *onset* of motion in a previously static object captures attention even though uninformative, independent of number of distractors; follow-up work (Abrams & Christ, "Motion onset really does capture attention," *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics*) and "Isoluminant motion onset captures attention" extended this to isoluminant motion.
- **Looming**: a 2010-2015 line of work from UCF (`stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/5929` "Do Looming Objects Capture Overt Attention?"; `stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/556` "Fixation Not Required: Characterizing Oculomotor Attention Capture for Looming Stimuli") found looming produces *covert* attentional capture (faster RTs/search costs) without necessarily requiring an overt fixation shift — i.e., looming draws attention largely through covert "behavioral urgency," not guaranteed saccades.
- **Saccade latency**: typical minimum saccadic reaction time to a new visual event is ~150-200 ms (general oculomotor literature, e.g. Carmi & Itti and `stars.library.ucf.edu`), consistent with voluntary saccade latencies of 200-250 ms cited in the smooth-pursuit search results above.
- **Capture-saccade probability**: not found as a single clean number for a full-field color flood; the closest evidence is that abrupt onsets reliably draw the first saccade in visual search paradigms (near-ceiling probability when the onset is the only new object: Yantis & Jonides), but this has not been quantified for a page-filling flood specifically. **Unverified** for exact probability/latency in a full-page-flood context.

**Scene-cut / global-change literature (closest analog to a "pour"):** Carmi & Itti (2006), "Visual causes versus correlates of attentional selection in dynamic scenes," *Vision Research* 46(26) (also ETRA 2006, `ilab.usc.edu/publications/Carmi_Itti06etra.html`), used MTV-style spliced clips and found that **dynamic models (motion, flicker) were most predictive specifically for saccades occurring immediately after a cut**, with minimal inter-observer variability right after the transition — i.e., a shared, synchronized orienting response right at a global visual change, decaying afterward. Mital et al. (2011), building the DIEM dataset, found strong **"attentional synchrony"** (tight clustering of gaze across viewers) tied to motion and scene cuts (`archive.pkmital.com` tag pages; see dataset entry below). Pannasch & Velichkovsky's ambient/focal work (`tu-dresden.de/.../pannasch2009.pdf`; `pannasch_vss_2014`) documents the general pattern: after a stimulus onset/scene cut/global change, fixation durations drop and saccade amplitudes increase (an "ambient" mode — fast, wide-ranging, low-level-feature-driven looking), then the system settles back into "focal" mode (longer fixations, shorter saccades, semantic/detail processing) over roughly the following second. Typical fixation durations are ~200-600 ms; saccade durations ~30-120 ms (these are general ranges, not pour-specific).

**Practical mapping for the gaze model**: on pour onset, add (a) a sharp transient pull of the predicted density toward the pour's origin point, peaking ~150-250 ms after onset and decaying over ~0.5-1 s as the front crosses the screen; (b) a general broadening/flattening of the density (ambient mode: shorter dwell, bigger jumps) for roughly the pour's duration plus ~0.5-1 s after; (c) no sustained pursuit term tracking the front edge itself.

## 2. Small continuous motion inside a static shape (the swirling letter-fill)

Weaker, more consistent evidence that motion draws fixations even at modest speed, but the magnitude is far below an onset/cut effect. Carmi & Itti's regression across seven bottom-up channels found **motion and flicker contrast were the strongest overall predictors of saccade targets in dynamic scenes, outperforming static channels (intensity/orientation/color contrast)**, which showed "particularly weak prediction accuracy" on their own (ilab.usc.edu summary above). In the deep video-saliency literature, **ACLNet** (Wang et al., "Revisiting Video Saliency: A Large-Scale Benchmark and a New Model," CVPR 2018, arXiv:1801.07424, dataset page `mmcheng.net/videosal/`) explicitly separates a static-saliency attention module (CNN, trained on image-saliency data) from a temporal module (convLSTM) specifically because static saliency alone under-predicts fixations on video; the paper reports consistent gains from adding the temporal/motion pathway on DHF1K, Hollywood-2 and UCF Sports, i.e. motion is a real, additive, but secondary signal layered on top of static salience, not a replacement for it. **Unverified**: no study isolates "slow, swirling, contained-within-a-static-boundary" motion specifically (vs. object motion or camera motion) — this is an inference from general motion-channel weight literature, not a direct test. The field strongly expects small-amplitude, low-speed, bounded motion to add a modest, continuous bias toward the moving region relative to equally salient static text, but not to compete with sudden onsets.

## 4. Webcam eye tracking feasibility

**WebGazer.js** (Papoutsaki et al., IJCAI 2016, `jeffhuang.com/papers/WebGazer_IJCAI16.pdf`): original paper reports ~175 px average error in lab conditions. Later independent evaluations (search results, not independently re-verified page-by-page): one 2023-era study reports calibration accuracy ~100-150 px (~2° visual angle) with post-validation error <1°; another online (non-lab, participant's own webcam) study reports mean error **3.65° (SD 0.77°)** with 15 participants; a further comparison against a low-cost commercial tracker reports **175-210 px / ~4.17°** error for WebGazer. Precision (RMS of consecutive samples) ~0.89° (SD 0.34°) in one 2024 study. Net: **WebGazer in the wild is roughly 3-4° / 150-250 px off**, i.e. usable for coarse region-of-interest (quadrant/large-UI-element) analysis but not for small-text fixation-level ground truth.

**Newer browser-based trackers (2025-2026, unverified beyond what search snippets state):**
- **WebEyeTrack** (2025, arXiv:2508.19544): on-device few-shot personalization (as few as 9 calibration samples), reports 2.32 cm error on GazeCapture, 2.4 ms inference on iPhone 14 — better than WebGazer but still device/head-pose sensitive.
- **EyeTheia** (2026, arXiv:2601.06279): MediaPipe landmarks + iTracker-style CNN, webcam-only, real-time; specific accuracy numbers not captured in the snippet — **unverified**, needs direct paper read.
- **GazeCapture / iTracker** (Krafka et al., CVPR 2016, arXiv:1606.05814, `gazecapture.csail.mit.edu`) is the standard large-scale benchmark (1450+ people, 2.5M frames): iTracker achieves **1.71 cm (phone) / 2.53 cm (tablet) without calibration**, **1.34 cm / 2.12 cm with calibration** — useful as an upper-bound reference for what a trained model can reach, though it's phone/tablet-camera, not generic laptop webcam.

**Recommendation for a small ground-truth set on the live site:** WebGazer.js (MIT-licensed, pure JS, no install) is the pragmatic choice for an opt-in in-page collector; expect ~3-4° (roughly 100-250 px at typical viewing distance) error without calibration, better with a short 9-point calibration. Treat any single-sample fixation estimate as region-level only; aggregate over repeated hovers/pours per participant to beat noise down. A dedicated webcam model (WebEyeTrack) is a stronger but heavier, less battle-tested alternative if cm-level accuracy matters.
